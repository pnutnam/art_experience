## 0. The artist and the magazine (context for all rooms)

**Joseph Ferdinand Keppler, b. Vienna Feb 1, 1838 — d. New York Feb 19, 1894 (56).**
[REF: Wikipedia after Dictionary of American Biography; Lambiek; NYU Keppler Family
Papers finding aid]

- Parents were bakers; talent first showed in **cake decorations**. Studied at the
  **Academy of Fine Arts Vienna**; earliest humorous drawings in the Viennese satirical
  sheet ***Kikeriki***. [REF: Wikipedia/DAB; Lambiek]
- Theater years: **scene painter and comedian** touring Tyrol and Italy; **restored old
  paintings in monasteries** — the theatrical training that later showed in his staged,
  costumed cartoons. Married actress Minna Rubens 1864 (d. 1870); married Pauline Pfau
  1871 (children: Udo, Irma, Olga). [REF: Wikipedia/DAB; Lambiek]
- Father left Vienna after the **failed 1848 revolution** → general store at **New
  Frankfort, northern Missouri**. ⚠ The 1848 motive is the standard account
  (Smithsonian: "due to his activities during the revolution of 1848") but thinly
  sourced beyond that. Joseph followed **1867** (with wife; "glowing accounts from
  America"). [REF: Smithsonian Libraries bio; Wikipedia]
- St. Louis: acting; satirical papers **Die Vehme** (1869, ~1 yr), **Frank und Frei**
  (~6 mo); first ***Puck*** **March 1871 – August 1872** (died within a year).
  East to New York fall 1872; drew for **Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper** from
  1874. [REF: Wikipedia; Lambiek; NYU finding aid]
- **Puck reborn:** German-language weekly **September 1876** with **Adolph
  Schwarzmann** (financial partner); **English edition March 14, 1877** ⚠ (some
  sources say March 7). Sixteen pages, ten cents ⚠ (Wikipedia currently prints "16
  cents," almost certainly a page-count typo; ten cents is corroborated by Spartacus
  Educational and Encyclopedia.com's "Ten-Cent Magazines" — the narration says
  "ten cents" on that basis). Chromolithographic front cover + back cover +
  double-page centerfold every week. [REF/SCH as noted]
- Circulation: **over 80,000/week by the early 1880s** [REF: GWU Special Collections];
  ⚠ reported spike to ~125,000 during the 1884 campaign [REF: Vulture 2014 — soft];
  ~90,000 in the 1890s [REF: JHU Muse]. Narration uses "more than eighty thousand."
- Politics: independent, anti-corruption, anti-Tammany, anti-monopoly, pro-civil-
  service reform; pro-Cleveland in 1884 ("its pro-Cleveland cartoons may well have
  contributed to the Democratic candidate's narrow victory" — U.S. Senate historians
  [MUS]). German-immigrant Forty-Eighter roots. **Complication, stated honestly in
  the tour:** vicious anti-Chinese imagery and anti-Catholic material even amid
  pro-immigration politics. [REF: Lambiek; Wikipedia; West]
- **vs. Thomas Nast** (the gallery's central contrast): Nast at Harper's Weekly in
  black-and-white **wood engraving** — hard line, moral war. Keppler: **lithography**
  (drawn on stone, painterly) and **color**. Senate historians [MUS]: Puck "employed
  lithography in place of wood engraving and offered three cartoons instead of the
  usual one… soon the magazine burst into full, eye-catching color." Nast preached;
  Keppler staged. [SCH: West, *Satire on Stone* (1988), the standard monograph;
  Hess & Kaplan, *The Ungentlemanly Art* (1968) — the founding survey; Fischer,
  *Them Damned Pictures* (1996); Brown, *Beyond the Lines* (2002) for the pictorial
  press world; Kahn & West, *What Fools These Mortals Be* (2014) — the rich
  catalog of the Billy Ireland exhibition (curated fall 2015–Jan 2016).]
- **Udo J. Keppler** (b. Apr 4, 1872 St. Louis — d. Jul 4, 1956): son; Puck art
  director ~1890–1913, co-owner; renamed himself **Joseph Keppler Jr.** after the
  father's death — the main attribution trap: "J. Keppler" post-1894 (e.g., "The
  Trust Titan's Point of View," 1900) is the son. Honorary Seneca chief
  (Do-ne-ho-ga-wa). [REF: Wikipedia; Lambiek]
- **The press:** Mayer, Merkel & Ottmann → from 1885 the **J. Ottmann Lithographing
  Company**; from 1886–87 in the **Puck Building** (295 Lafayette St., at Houston/
  Mulberry; Albert & Herman Wagner) — "the world's largest lithographic presswork
  under a single roof" per contemporary guides; own dynamo, ~300 employees,
  30,000–60,000 impressions/day. **Book conservator Jeff Peachey's 2017 survey found
  lithographic stones and two flywheels still on site.** ⚠ The popular line that
  "stones lie buried under the Houston Street sidewalk" is the romantic version —
  the verified fact is "still on site"; narration says "still down there, where the
  presses stood." [MUS/REF: King's Handbook of New York (1892); editions.studio;
  Peachey (2017)]
- **Chromolithography** (craft context for every room): one polished Bavarian
  limestone per color, drawn/translated by hand, printed in registration; a
  specialist "chromist" separated colors; typical chromolithographs used **8–40
  stones** [MUS: American Antiquarian Society]; chromos ran to **twenty colors and
  more** [SCH: Peter C. Marzio, *The Democratic Art: Pictures for a 19th-Century
  America — Chromolithography 1840–1900*, Godine 1979 — the standard history; his
  phrase "the democratic art" names the whole enterprise]. ⚠ No stone count is
  documented for any specific Puck centerfold — narration describes the method
  without a number.

## 1. ROOM ONE — "SHEOL" (Puck, v. 17, no. 429, May 27, 1885, centerfold)

**Record [PRIM]:** LOC LCCN 2011661750; LC-DIG-ppmsca-28201; "1 print :
chromolithograph. N.Y.: Published by Keppler & Schwarzmann, 1885 May 27. Illus. from
Puck, v. 17, no. 429, centerfold. Copyright 1885 by Keppler & Schwarzmann."
https://www.loc.gov/item/2011661750/ — no known restrictions.

**The premise (the joke's machinery):**
- The **English Revised Version** — the only authorized revision of the KJV —
  commissioned 1870 (Convocation of Canterbury); company of 50+ scholars, British and
  American (Philip Schaff among the Americans; Westcott & Hort best known).
  **NT: May 17, 1881. OT: May 1885 (on sale May 17–19).** [PRIM: RV preface via
  bible-researcher.com; SCH literature on the RV]
- **Sheol** occurs ~64–65× in the OT ⚠ (count varies by 1 across sources); KJV renders
  it **hell ~31×, grave ~31×, pit 3×** [REF: Concise Bible Dictionary]; the English
  revisers **transliterated "Sheol" 29 of 64 times** [PRIM: ASV Preface verbatim —
  "The English Revisers use 'Sheol' twenty-nine times out of the sixty-four"]; the
  American committee preferred uniform "Sheol," carried out in the ASV (1901). NT
  (1881) had already transliterated "Hades" ~10×.
- The RV preface's own reason, quotable [PRIM]: "hell" — though "a fairly adequate
  equivalent" in its older creedal sense — "is so commonly understood of the place of
  torment" that keeping it "would lead to inevitable misunderstanding."
- **The gap: OT on sale May 19, 1885 → cartoon dated May 27, 1885 — eight days.**
  Near-journalistic speed for a chromolithographic centerfold.
- **Puck's printed caption, verbatim [PRIM]:** "According to the new version of the
  Old Testament, many respectable people who have been writhing in the old fashioned
  Hell will have to be transferred to the pleasant watering-place known as 'Sheol.'
  This is Puck's notion of the evolution of Hell to Sheol." — the word
  **"evolution"** is a pun, with Darwin visibly aboard.
- Puck editorially approved [REF: Darwin Project gloss]: Sheol "is not Hell, but
  simply the abode of the dead, the Hades of the Greeks." Keppler returned to the
  vein in 1888 ("Rapid Transit to Sheol," after a sensational Trinity Church sermon —
  Cornell P.J. Mode collection) and had parked a Tammany boss in "Hades" in 1881.

**The cast [PRIM: LOC figure list — verified verbatim]:** Hypatia, Fanny Elssler,
Voltaire, Frederick the Great, Socrates, J. Offenbach, Darwin, J.S. Mill, Rousseau,
George Sand, Galileo, Jefferson, Th. Paine, Goethe, H. Heine — ferried by **Charon**.
Why a pulpit's "damned": freethinkers, heretics, skeptics, a dancer, Jews, a
"corrupter of youth." Fresh arrivals: **Elssler d. Nov 27, 1884** (six months);
**Darwin d. Apr 19, 1882** (three years); **Offenbach d. Oct 5, 1880** (his
*Orpheus in the Underworld*, 1858, had already made Hades comic). Detail from the
Darwin Project catalogue: **Darwin sits alone by a tree with J.S. Mill leaning
against the same tree**; lovers walking from the wood = **Paolo and Francesca**
(Dante). ⚠ The pig beside Offenbach is in the image; its meaning (Orphic in-joke,
picnic roast, satire on his critics) is **interpretation, not record** — narration
mentions only the sheet music. ⚠ A "TO SHEOL!" roadside arrow appears at the
water's edge per image inspection, less sharply documented than the main sign.
- **Van Wyhe (Darwin Online) [SCH]:** "Puck treated Darwin as a hero" (contra the
  San Francisco Wasp); catalogues this print; Puck had drawn Darwin in 1883 too.

**The picture (composition map; normalized x,y from left/top):**
- **Upper left (x≈0.04–0.14, y≈0.05–0.20): the Devil** — horns, folded bat wings,
  chin-in-hand thinker pose, dark blue-grey; furnaces dying; **great double gate
  chained and padlocked**; bats; **the sign [PRIM, verified by crop]:** "THIS BUSINESS
  IS REMOVED TO SHEOL, OPPOSITE" — an American eviction notice, not a medieval curse.
- **Lower left into center (x≈0.15–0.35, y≈0.5–0.75): Charon's ferry**, bow pointing
  right, toward the light; passengers labeled (DARWIN legible at survey zoom).
- **Center/right (x≈0.5–0.85): the watering place** — bandstand, dancers on the lawn
  (Elssler at work in white), strollers, grand pavilion/rotunda on the hill, temple
  grove, sunlit water. **The border between worlds is light itself** — cold blues
  (abandoned hell) vs warm gold (Sheol).
- Bottom caption strip with the printed caption.

**Traditions a curator would invoke [SCH/MUS iconographic standards]:** the classical
**Elysian Fields**; **Watteau's *Embarkation for Cythera*** (lovers boarding a ferry
to a pleasure island — Charon's boat inverted into a picnic steamer); the Victorian
"watering place" (spa/resort culture); the dark left corner descends from **Gustave
Doré's** engraved underworlds (Dante 1861; Paradise Lost 1866).

**Curator's slow-looking list:** the padlock and chain; the Devil's bankrupt-
shopkeeper slump; the sign's deadpan; the dying embers by the waterline; Elssler's
fresh grave (six months); Darwin's solitude beside Mill; the lovers and the goat;
Offenbach's music; the chromatic seam where smoke meets sunshine.

**Socratic angles used in the tour:** first-looking (VTS opening); the Devil as
failed businessman (what happened to him?); who's on the boat and who's missing;
who would YOU ferry; what the picture asks of you this week.

## 2. ROOM TWO — THE RETURN OF THE "PRODIGAL FATHER" (Puck, v. 14, no. 344, Oct 10, 1883, centerfold)

**Record [PRIM]:** LOC LCCN 92520934; LC-DIG-ppmsca-28432; chromolithograph;
artist credit "Keppler, Joseph Ferdinand, 1838–1894, artist" (self-caricature —
attribution rock-solid). https://www.loc.gov/item/92520934/

**The scene [PRIM: LOC description, verified]:** the returning editor mobbed by his
staff; **the Rev. T. DeWitt Talmage** — then Brooklyn's most famous preacher and a
favorite Puck target — waving adieu from the doorway; the **"tormentors of the
editor"** identifiable among the staff (**Frederick Burr Opper** — future giant of
American cartooning — J.A. Mitchell, Tom Merritt, H.S. Taylor, Gillam, C. Taylor,
Zimm…, J.A. Wales); brigands returning loot; a French cook; **the goat at the
luggage** (bottom left, gnawing the valise). "A prodigal son gets the fatted calf;
a prodigal father, apparently, gets dessert" (tour's line).

**What it is:** Keppler's homecoming from his 1883 European trip, drawn by himself —
the gallery's introduction to its maker, as a gag. Biographical beats used in
narration are all from §0 (baker's son → Academy → theater → 1848 → St. Louis →
failed papers → 1876/77 Puck).

**Nast contrast beat:** see §0. The Senate historians' sentence and the
"three color cartoons a week" point are the documented spine [MUS].

**Socratic angles:** why a satirist draws himself as the joke (armor/trust/
invitation); where the visitor gets their truth told and how they take it.

## 3. ROOM THREE — THE BOSSES OF THE SENATE (Puck, Jan 23, 1889, centerfold)

**Record [PRIM]:** LOC LCCN **2002718861** (⚠ the numbers floating around the web —
2012645272, 2012645381 — are unrelated prints; 2002718861 is the real record);
"Lith. by J. Ottmann after drawing by J. Keppler"; chromolithograph; digital
cph.3b52004 / LC-USZC4-494; no known restrictions.
https://www.loc.gov/item/2002718861/

**The picture:** bloated, human-headed **money bags** in top hats — **Standard Oil,
Copper, Sugar, Iron, Steel Beam, Tin, Coal, Paper Bag** trusts — looming over the
pygmy senators of the **50th Congress** at their desks; banner overhead parodying
Lincoln at Gettysburg: **"This is a Senate of the Monopolists, by the Monopolists,
and for the Monopolists"**; door labeled **"People's Entrance"** — **bolted and
barred** (left edge, upper-middle band; verified by high-res crop). Murky
umber/green institutional palette; no angry face anywhere — "the anger is yours."

**Standing:**
- U.S. Senate historians [MUS, archived]: "a frequently reproduced cartoon, long a
  staple of textbooks and studies of Congress"; it "reflected the phenomenal growth
  of American industry in the 1880s, but also the disturbing trend toward
  concentration of industry."
- **[PEER] Richard R. John, "Robber Barons Redux: Antimonopoly Reconsidered,"
  *Enterprise & Society* 13:1 (March 2012), 1–38** — open access (Columbia Academic
  Commons, DOI 10.7916/D8NK4XMJ); keeps antimonopoly — with this cartoon in its
  visual file — a live scholarly category, against Chandler-era business-history
  dismissals of "robber baron" framing.
- **Sherman Antitrust Act, July 2, 1890** — a year and a half after the cartoon;
  the cartoon is routinely cited as crystallizing the public mood the Act answered
  (e.g., Wikipedia's antitrust-law article uses it as the era's image).
- ⚠ **DO NOT USE (unverified, despite wide retelling):** that Standard Oil later
  reprinted the cartoon approvingly; that Abram Hewitt inspired it; NPR's exact
  "defining image of late 19th-century Washington" phrasing (the sentiment is real,
  the verbatim quote is not pinned — narration hedges with "has been called").
- The money-bag man as a stock figure of Gilded Age satire — cf. **[SCH] Hess &
  Kaplan (1968)** and **Fischer (1996)** for the trope's lineage; Keppler's
  contribution is the **seating chart** (the trusts inside the chamber) and the
  **sacred-text swap** (one word of Gettysburg — a device he used repeatedly).

**Socratic angles:** where the eye lands first (scale = power physics); the bolted
door — who goes through first if you have the key; where the bolted door is *now*;
what the picture asks of you (allowed: nothing).

## 4. ROOM FOUR — PUCK'S OWN YORKTOWN CELEBRATION (Puck, v. 10, no. 241, Oct 19, 1881, centerfold)

**Record [PRIM]:** LOC LCCN 2012647294; LC-DIG-ppmsca-28522; chromolithograph;
"Keppler, Joseph Ferdinand, 1838–1894, artist." https://www.loc.gov/item/2012647294/

**The occasion:** Yorktown Centennial, October 1881 — 100th anniversary of
Cornwallis's surrender; warships, French and German delegations, the monument under
construction. **Caption, verbatim [PRIM]:** "His army of contributors passing in
review before our foreign guests."

**The parade, from the LOC description [PRIM]:** **Jay Gould and Vanderbilt marching
as a visiting emperor and king**, attended by **Cyrus Field, Brady, Keene, Russell
Sage** bowing; **star-route robbers led by Dorsey**; **assessment robbers led by
Hubbell**; **Robeson**; on white horses the **presidential possibilities — Grant,
Conkling, Cameron, Logan, Sherman, Hoar, Edmunds, Beck**; **stalwart Republicans**
arm in arm with **"Rum, Romanism, and Rebellion"**; **Butler's tumble-bug**
industriously working; the **Tammany tiger**; **little giant McConnell**; **Jefferson
Davis flying the stars and bars**; **"chilling music" by Vance, Beck(?), Morgan(?),
Gorham** [read the LOC text for exact names]; reviewing stand: **Hon. William M.
Evarts** bowing as guest of honor; **Cornelius Vanderbilt in a box with "handsome
females"** (the period's royalty as spectators); monument at left; **the devil and
his wife on a cloud, "on their way to Europe"** (right of the banner, upper right).
**Two images of Puck** in the drawing: the sprite on horseback leading the column
and again behind the bugler. ⚠ The marching-order details beyond the LOC text are
image-reading, cited to the record where possible.

**The mascot:** from Shakespeare's *A Midsummer Night's Dream* (the sprite of
Act 3, Scene 2 — in the play, "Lord, what fools these mortals be!"); Puck's figure:
a chubby top-hatted trickster admiring himself in a hand mirror; cast-iron statues
by **Henry Baerer** still crown the Puck Building (landmarked 1983). [MUS/REF]

**The ending (used in narration):** 1893 — Keppler produces the special **World's
Fair Puck** at the Chicago Columbian Exposition; the strain ruins his health; dies
Feb 19, 1894, of heart disease. Puck continues under Udo ( Hearst buys 1916; last
issue Sept 5, 1918). The stones Peachey found are still on site. Final beat: the
tour closes on the Devil-as-tourist — Keppler's universe looping from Room One.

**Socratic angles:** who gets a place in the parade (his fame = a parade of the
people he caught); what your own life's parade looks like; what to keep from the
gallery.

## 5. The method (how the guide asks)

- **Visual Thinking Strategies [SCH]:** Abigail Housen (aesthetic-development
  research, thousands of recorded looking interviews) & Philip Yenawine (MoMA
  educator), *Visual Thinking Strategies: Using Art to Deepen Learning Across School
  Disciplines*, Harvard Education Press, 2013. Protocol questions, verbatim:
  **"What's going on in this picture? What do you see that makes you say that?
  What more can we find?"** Also powers the NYT's weekly "What's Going On in This
  Picture?"
- **Slow looking [SCH/REF]:** Jennifer L. Roberts, "The Power of Patience,"
  Harvard Magazine, Nov–Dec 2013 — verbatim: **"Just because you have looked at
  something doesn't mean that you have seen it"**; her students spend three full
  hours with one painting ("The time span is explicitly designed to seem excessive");
  nine minutes to notice Copley's ear echoing the squirrel's ruff. Slow Art Day:
  founded 2009 by Phil Terry (Jewish Museum epiphany); ~5 works × 10 minutes +
  discussion, hundreds of venues each April. [MUS: slowartday.com]
- **Gallery Socratic tradition [SCH]:** Rika Burnham & Elliott Kai-Kee, *Teaching in
  the Art Museum* (Getty, 2011); George Hein, *Learning in the Museum* (1998).
- **Quotable critics (used for tone, not narration):** Kenneth Clark, *Looking at
  Pictures* (1960) — "art must do something more than give pleasure"; Jerry Saltz
  (VICE 2015) on looking bodily and from many distances; the O'Keeffe line he
  cherishes — "If you take a flower in your hand and really look at it, it's your
  world for a moment." ⚠ Sister Wendy's "The only way to look at a picture is
  slowly, and with love" is aggregator-sourced; approach, not quote, is used.
